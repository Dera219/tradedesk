# Short Selling

## What short selling is

Short selling is betting that a stock will fall. You borrow shares from your broker, sell them at
the current price, and aim to buy them back later at a lower price to return them — keeping the
difference. If the stock falls, you profit; if it rises, you lose.

## Why the risk is different from buying

When you buy a stock, the most you can lose is what you paid — the price can only fall to zero. A
short is the reverse and the asymmetry is dangerous: a stock can rise without limit, so a short
position's loss is theoretically unlimited. A short that moves against you gets *larger* as it
worsens, which is the opposite of a normal position.

## Borrow cost and recall

Borrowed shares are not free. You pay a borrow fee for as long as the short is open, and for
hard-to-borrow stocks that fee can be steep. The lender can also **recall** the shares, forcing
you to close the position at a moment not of your choosing.

## The short squeeze

If a heavily shorted stock starts rising, short sellers rushing to buy back shares to cap their
losses add buying pressure, which pushes the price up further, which forces more shorts to cover.
This feedback loop is a **short squeeze**, and it can drive a price far above anything justified by
the business, fast. It is the mechanism behind several dramatic stock episodes.
